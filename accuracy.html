What a score out of 100 actually means

Think of each figure below as a grade for one drum part. The engine is judged on every real hit in the reference recording, and it can fail in two ways: it can miss a hit, or it can invent one that is not there. Both mistakes cost it the same, which means it cannot inflate its grade by guessing extra notes to cover the gaps.

A score of 100 would mean it found every real hit and added nothing that was not played. A score of 91 means that for every hundred real hits, the misses and the false hits add up to nine. That is the number drummers care about: how much of your part shows up on screen, and how much is junk.

If you know the field: that balanced score is called F1. It is the standard way to report detection results in machine learning precisely because a single “accuracy” figure can be gamed by guessing, and F1 cannot.

Note Detection

How often the engine finds a real drum hit and files it under the right piece of the kit. Each instrument has its own model, trained on that instrument's sound — which is why a kick is near-perfect and the three toms are the hardest.

🥁 Snare 90%
🎧 Hi-Hat 81%
🎵 Ride 79%
💥 Crash 78%
🥁 Floor Tom 67%
🥁 Mid Tom 59%
🥁 High Tom 56%

A hit counts as correct when it lands within a tenth of a second (± 100 ms) of the real one — tight enough that a note can never look visibly late on the highway. Kick and snare, the backbone of every groove, are found most reliably; hi-hat and cymbals follow. The three toms are the toughest part of any kit to tell apart, so a tom hit is usually found but sometimes filed under the wrong tom — the note is there, the label is what slips.

Tempo Detection

A chart is only playable if its beat grid lines up with the music, so tempo is judged separately from note detection. The detector sees only when notes fall — never the answer it is asked to produce.

Percentages are the share of songs where the detector got the answer exactly right. Two frozen held-out song sets, never used for any tuning decision. “16th grid” is the grid a chart actually draws, with every beat line fixed from the start of the song rather than nudged to fit.

The two sets above are separate batches of charted songs, each with a known authored tempo, so you can see the figures hold up on data the engine never touched. One limit deserves to be stated plainly: about one song in six comes back at half or double time. That is not a bug being passed off as a feature — a beat heard in sixteenths and the same beat heard in eighths produce identical hits, so nothing in the note timing can decide between them. Given the right half or double, the engine nails the tempo 84% of the time. Rather than hide that behind a flattering number, we publish it.

How These Numbers Are Produced

The method matters as much as the result, so here is exactly what is and is not being claimed.

  • The reference is checked by a human. Before anything is graded, a person corrects the reference annotations — so a mislabelled crash is not counted against the engine.
  • Timing is graded with a tolerance. A hit counts as correct if it lands within a tenth of a second of the real one. Every hit in every song is pooled into one score, so the result is not skewed by a handful of short tracks.
  • Every song is one the engine had never heard. Nothing above was in the training data. Where a setting had to be chosen, it was picked on one half of the songs and graded on the other half.
  • The measured model is the one you get. These scores come from the exact model running in production, not from a stronger version that was tried once and never shipped.
  • Tempo is detected without being told the answer. The tempo detector never sees the chart's existing tempo map — it works out the beat from note timing alone.
  • Figures are rounded down, never up. A 0.9058 is published as 91, not 91.5 or 92. We would rather quote a hard 69% for exact tempo than the flattering 94% that describes how well the grid fits instead.
